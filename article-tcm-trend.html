TCM, TikTok, and Soft Power

Is the Viral Wellness Trend a Form of Cultural Influence or Just Consumerism? | Autumn 2026

Scroll through TikTok or Instagram for five minutes, and you will see it. A young woman in Brooklyn boiling goji berries. A British influencer applying moxibustion patches. A Chinese American blogger explaining why you should drink hot water. Traditional Chinese Medicine has taken over social media, and Gen Z is driving the trend.

But this is not just a wellness story. It is also a story about cultural influence, soft power, and the global reception of Chinese traditions — themes that sit at the heart of China Studies. This article examines whether the TCM trend reflects China's growing cultural reach, or whether it is simply a consumer-driven phenomenon stripped of its deeper meaning.

"TCM is not just trending. It is a case study in China's cultural influence."

The Numbers Are Staggering

The data speaks for itself. On TikTok alone, #GuaSha has accumulated over 4.7 billion views, while acupuncture-related content has surpassed 2 billion views. The #TCM tag hosts more than 230,000 videos. One creator, Sherry Zhu, saw her video on "sipping hot water throughout the day" rack up over 5.5 million views. Another, oldtown_acupuncturist, has built a following of over 580,000 people through acupuncture demonstrations and TCM education.

These are not niche numbers. They represent a mainstream cultural moment — one that raises important questions about how Chinese culture is being received and reinterpreted globally.

What Is Soft Power — and Why Does TCM Matter?

Soft power, a concept coined by political scientist Joseph Nye, refers to a country's ability to influence others through culture, values, and policies, rather than through coercion or payment. China has invested heavily in soft power — through Confucius Institutes, cultural exchanges, and its "cultural confidence" policy — but some of its most effective soft power has emerged organically from the grassroots, often through platforms like TikTok and Xiaohongshu.

TCM is a particularly interesting case study. It is a deeply embedded part of Chinese civilisation, with roots in Daoist philosophy, herbal medicine, and holistic healing. Its sudden popularity on Western social media raises the question: is this a genuine cultural exchange, or is it a form of cultural extraction — where the aesthetics of TCM are consumed without the underlying philosophy?

The Three Engines Driving the Trend

The online TCM boom is driven by three distinct types of content, each with its own relationship to Chinese culture.

1. "Chinamaxxing" — An Earnest Engagement

Young Westerners are actively imitating Chinese daily habits. Boiling water. Wearing slippers indoors. Drinking warm beverages. Eating congee for breakfast. The trend has a name: "Chinamaxxing." It is playful, self-aware, and surprisingly earnest.

What is notable here is that this is not driven by state actors or official cultural promotion. It is an organic, ground-up engagement with Chinese lifestyle practices, driven by curiosity and a search for alternatives to Western norms. This is soft power from below — and it is arguably more effective than any official campaign.

2. "Chinese Wellness" — Reinvented for Social Media

Traditional wellness practices are being reinvented for social media. On Xiaohongshu (China's Instagram equivalent), simple posts like "TCM advice: boil apple water" can get over 100,000 likes. AI-generated videos of ancient physicians like Bian Que and Hua Tuo "come back to life" to give health tips, mixing humour with genuine advice. TCM night markets, where young people get free pulse diagnoses and acupuncture demos, have become weekend destinations in cities like Zhengzhou and Chengdu.

This is a hybrid form of cultural transmission — where ancient wisdom is remixed for a digital, global audience. It is both authentic and adapted, a kind of cultural translation that makes TCM accessible without necessarily preserving its full philosophical depth.

3. The "Chinese Baddie" Aesthetic — Style Over Substance?

This is the visual side of the trend. Think dewy skin, minimalist makeup, elegant clothing, and a lifestyle that looks calm and put together. Influencers like Sherry Zhu pioneered this aesthetic. Her video on hot water drinking got over 5.5 million views and made her a face of the movement.

But here lies the tension: is this cultural appreciation or cultural extraction? The "Chinese Baddie" aesthetic draws heavily from Chinese visual culture, but often without reference to its philosophical or historical roots. It is the most commercialised aspect of the trend — and the most distant from the tradition it draws from.

Soft Power or Consumer Power?

China's official soft power strategy has long promoted TCM as a key pillar of cultural confidence. The state has supported TCM research, international exhibitions, and the establishment of TCM clinics abroad. Yet the current trend is largely organic, driven by creators, algorithms, and consumer demand — not by state policy.

This raises an important question: does a trend driven primarily by consumer power count as soft power? If the appeal of TCM is reduced to viral aesthetics and detox teas, then it is perhaps better described as consumer power — China's culture being consumed as a product, rather than understood as a tradition.

For soft power to be meaningful, it requires not just visibility, but understanding and respect for the culture behind the practice. The TCM trend succeeds on visibility, but often fails on depth. This is the tension at the heart of the phenomenon.

What Experts Say

Academics and practitioners have offered varied perspectives on the trend. Dr. Felice Chan, a London-based acupuncturist and doctor of Chinese medicine, has welcomed the visibility, telling The Guardian that "if it gives acupuncturists jobs, if it gives light to other Chinese medicine brands, why not?" However, she has also raised concerns about cultural extraction, noting that TCM is often reduced to "a mask" for a "quick fix."

Chen Xuguang of Peking University has linked the trend to China's growing cultural confidence, framing it as part of a broader search for holistic meaning in an increasingly fragmented world. Meanwhile, a 2025 Lancet editorial highlighted the tension between integration and risk, noting that TCM's growing popularity "could be a major opportunity or a major risk" for modern healthcare.

What unites these perspectives is the recognition that TCM is no longer a niche practice — it has become a global cultural phenomenon. Whether that is a form of soft power, cultural exchange, or simply consumerism is a question that remains open.

The Commercialisation of Culture

Social media buzz has translated into real revenue. Nin Jiom Pei Pa Koa (cough syrup) generates 635 million yuan in annual overseas sales. ULucky (moxibustion heating belt) hit a peak daily revenue of 400,000 yuan. WTHN, a London-based TCM brand, secured $5 million in investment from LVMH.

This commercialisation raises uncomfortable questions. When a philosophical system becomes a product, is its meaning preserved or eroded? The answer is likely both. Commercialisation has made TCM accessible to a global audience — but it has also stripped it of context, reducing it to a set of consumable practices.

The Skeptics' View

  • "It is just consumerism." TCM-themed cafes charge £6 for goji lattes. The same corporations that sold you fast fashion now sell you "ancient wisdom."
  • "It is cultural appropriation." TCM is a complete philosophical system, not just a collection of products. Reducing it to "hot water and goji berries" risks stripping it of meaning.
  • "The evidence is weak." A 2025 Lancet study found that only 20% of TCM therapies have strong clinical evidence.

Why It Might Stick

Despite the skepticism, TCM's online appeal has staying power. It offers control when healthcare feels impersonal. It offers connection to tradition in an age of loneliness. And crucially, it offers a window into Chinese culture — one that is accessible, visual, and engaging, even if imperfect.

For China Studies students, this trend is worth watching — not just as a wellness fad, but as a case study in how Chinese culture is being received, adapted, and sometimes misunderstood in the globalised world.

What This Tells Us About China and the World

The TCM trend is not just about wellness. It is about how Chinese culture travels across borders, how it is consumed and reimagined by global audiences, and how tradition and commercialisation intersect in the digital age.

Whether this is a form of soft power, cultural exchange, or consumerism, one thing is clear: China's traditions are no longer confined to China. They are being discovered, debated, and reshaped by a generation that is searching for meaning, connection, and alternatives to the world they inherited.
